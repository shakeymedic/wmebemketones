Understanding Ketone Bodies

Ketones are produced when the body metabolises fat for energy. There are three main types, and how we measure them matters.

The Ketones

  • Beta-hydroxybutyrate (BOHB): The most abundant ketone in ketoacidosis.
  • Acetoacetate (AcAc): The next most common.
  • Acetone: Less abundant, responsible for the 'fruity' breath smell.

Measurement Methods

  • Capillary (Point-of-Care): Measures BOHB. Preferred method for ED diagnosis and monitoring.
  • Laboratory (Serum): Measures BOHB. Gold standard but slower turnaround.
  • Urine Dipstick: Measures Acetoacetate only. Can be falsely reassuring as it misses the main ketone (BOHB). Not recommended for DKA diagnosis or monitoring.

Clinical Pearl

Always trust a capillary or lab ketone reading over a urine dipstick. A negative urine test does not exclude significant ketoacidosis.

Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

A life-threatening complication of diabetes (usually Type 1), characterised by a triad of hyperglycaemia, ketonaemia, and acidaemia.

Assessment

  • Polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss
  • Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
  • Kussmaul breathing (deep, sighing)
  • Pear-drop smell on breath (ketones)
  • Dehydration signs (tachycardia, hypotension)
  • Altered mental status, confusion, coma
  • Look for precipitants (infection, missed insulin, MI)

Diagnosis (The Triad)

  • Capillary Blood Glucose > 11 mmol/L (or known diabetes)
  • Capillary/Blood Ketones > 3 mmol/L
  • Bicarbonate < 15 mmol/L and/or Venous pH < 7.3

NB: Euglycaemic DKA (eDKA) can occur with glucose < 11 mmol/L, especially in patients on SGLT-2 inhibitors.

Severity: Mild (pH 7.2-7.29), Moderate (pH 7.1-7.19), Severe (pH < 7.1)

Treatment (Follow Local Trust Guidelines)

  1. Fluid Resuscitation: 0.9% Saline. 1L stat, then follow protocol (e.g., 1L over 2h, 1L over 4h).
  2. Fixed Rate Insulin Infusion (FRII): 0.1 units/kg/hour. **Do not stop long-acting insulin.**
  3. Potassium Replacement: Crucial. Add KCl to fluids based on K+ level. Start early unless K+ > 5.5.
  4. Monitor Closely: Hourly capillary ketones & glucose. VBG at 1h, 2h, then 2-4 hourly.
  5. Consider HDU/ITU if severe: pH < 7.1, GCS < 12, persistent hypotension.

Alcoholic Ketoacidosis (AKA)

Occurs in patients with chronic alcohol use, typically after a binge followed by a period of poor oral intake ("feed-fast" cycle). Dehydration and depleted glycogen stores lead to ketosis.

Assessment

  • Recent alcohol binge, now stopped
  • Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain
  • May present similarly to DKA or pancreatitis
  • Tachycardia, tachypnoea, dehydration
  • Often co-existent gastritis, pancreatitis, or liver disease

Diagnosis

  • Metabolic acidosis with raised anion gap
  • Positive serum/urine ketones
  • Glucose is variable: can be low, normal, or mildly elevated (rarely >15 mmol/L).
  • Alcohol level may be low or undetectable.
  • Lactate may be mildly raised.

Treatment

  1. Thiamine: Give Pabrinex (or IV Thiamine) **BEFORE** any glucose-containing fluids.
  2. Fluid & Glucose: Start with 0.9% Saline. Once haemodynamically stable, switch to Dextrose 5% with added KCl. Dextrose stimulates endogenous insulin release, which switches off ketogenesis.
  3. Electrolyte Replacement: Correct hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, and hypophosphataemia.
  4. Anti-emetics & supportive care.
  5. **Insulin is NOT usually required.**

Starvation Ketosis

A physiological response to prolonged fasting or very low carbohydrate intake. The body switches to fat metabolism, producing ketones as an alternative fuel source. Not typically associated with severe acidosis.

Assessment

  • History of poor oral intake, fasting, fad diets (e.g., ketogenic), or vomiting (e.g., hyperemesis gravidarum).
  • General malaise, weakness, lightheadedness.
  • Symptoms are usually mild compared to DKA/AKA.
  • Often an incidental finding on bloods.

Diagnosis

  • Mild ketonaemia (ketones often < 3 mmol/L).
  • pH is usually normal or near-normal (> 7.3).
  • Bicarbonate is often normal or only slightly low (> 18 mmol/L).
  • Glucose is typically low or low-normal.
  • Anion gap is normal or only slightly elevated.

Treatment

  1. Restore Oral Intake: Encourage carbohydrate-containing food and drink.
  2. IV Fluids if needed: If unable to tolerate oral intake, use Dextrose-containing fluids (e.g., Dextrose 5% or Dextrose Saline).
  3. Treat underlying cause: e.g., anti-emetics for vomiting.
  4. Insulin is not required.

eDKA & Other Ketone Causes

While DKA, AKA, and starvation account for most cases, it's crucial to be aware of important mimics and variants.

Euglycaemic DKA (eDKA)

A dangerous DKA variant where blood glucose is not significantly elevated (often < 14 mmol/L), meeting other criteria for DKA (ketones >3, pH <7.3).

Key Triggers:

  • SGLT-2 Inhibitors: (e.g., Dapagliflozin, Empagliflozin). These drugs cause glycosuria, masking hyperglycaemia.
  • Pregnancy
  • Reduced carbohydrate intake or fasting
  • Cocaine use

Clinical Action: Any patient on an SGLT-2 inhibitor who is unwell (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain) needs a capillary ketone check, regardless of their blood glucose level.

Other Causes of Ketosis

Consider these in the acidotic patient when the cause is unclear:

  • Salicylate Overdose: Causes a mixed respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis. Ketones can be present.
  • Isopropanol Ingestion: Isopropanol is metabolised to acetone, leading to ketosis without acidosis.
  • Inborn Errors of Metabolism: A rare consideration, primarily in children.
